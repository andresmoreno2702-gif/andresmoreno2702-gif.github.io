The idea was simple: build an RC car where I designed every part — chassis geometry, remote body, firmware. I started in Fusion 360, modelling the suspension geometry before I had even ordered the wheels. The chassis came out properly: four rubber tyres, a servo-steered Ackermann front axle, and a rear-wheel drive layout.

The remote was the part I was most proud of. Instead of using an off-the-shelf transmitter, I modelled a gamepad from scratch, 3D-printed the shell in two halves, fitted two analog joystick modules inside, and ran everything through an ESP32 WROOM using ESP32-NOW. The latency was good. The control feel was actually decent.

What went wrong: I kept adding things. A 5A boost converter to push 12V to the motor, an oversized 3500mAh LiPo, a buck converter, the motor driver — each module seemed justified at the time. By the time it was all assembled and bolted down, the car worked but moved like it was carrying twice its own weight. The DRV8876 and motor were fine. The problem was the weight. The lesson stuck — directly led to the small RC car, which uses none of the excess.

Build

Step 01
Chassis design in Fusion 360
Started with the wheel geometry — real rubber tyres with a proper tread pattern — and worked backwards to figure out the chassis dimensions. Modelled a working Ackermann steering linkage, front and rear axles, servo mount, and motor housing. Everything was parametric so I could adjust wheelbase without redrawing from scratch.
Differential gear assembly — 3D-printed components
Step 02
3D printing the chassis
Printed in black PLA on a Creality printer. The main chassis plate, servo mount bracket, rear motor housing, and wheel hubs all came out cleanly. The Ackermann arms took a couple of iterations to get the right pivot geometry — first print was too flexible at the steering knuckle.
Chassis — servo, rubber tyres, rear drive motor
Step 03
Electronics — ESP32 WROOM + DRV8876
The car runs an ESP32 WROOM as the receiver. It listens for ESP32-NOW packets from the remote and maps joystick values to PWM signals — left joystick Y-axis for throttle/reverse, right joystick X-axis for servo steering angle. The DRV8876 handles motor direction and PWM speed control via IN1/IN2 mode. Power came from a WLtoys 144001 2S 7.4V 3500mAh LiPo — the 3A buck converter stepped it down to 5V for the ESP32 and electronics, while the 5A boost converter pushed 12V directly to the motor. That boost converter was the first mistake — more voltage didn't fix what was fundamentally a weight problem.
Electronics assembly — ESP32 WROOM, DRV8876, LiPo wiring
Step 04
Assembly and first drive
Getting everything fitted into the chassis without shorting anything was the hardest part. With a buck converter, a boost converter, the DRV8876 driver, and the oversized LiPo, the electronics stack sat on top of the frame rather than inside it — raising the centre of mass. First drive test confirmed the car worked correctly: steering responsive, throttle smooth, ESP32-NOW had no noticeable lag. But it was slow — way slower than expected for the motor spec. The weight was the culprit.

Custom Remote

Designed from scratch
Gamepad remote
Instead of using an off-the-shelf transmitter I modelled a full gamepad shell in Fusion 360. The body was split into two printable halves, designed to fit exactly around two KY-023 analog joystick modules and an ESP32 WROOM board. Printed in black PLA with hand-fit tolerances — no fasteners for the shell, just press-fit clips. The joystick thumb-caps are custom 3D-modelled covers that screw on and off, allowing the sticks to be swapped or adjusted without disassembling the remote.
  • Modelled in Fusion 360, printed on Creality
  • Dual KY-023 analog joystick modules
  • ESP32 WROOM transmitter
  • ESP32-NOW protocol — no pairing, peer-to-peer MAC
  • 3.7V 18650 3000mAh 20C Li-ion cell, fits inside grip section
  • Screw-on/off 3D-printed joystick thumb-cap covers

What I Learned

Lesson 01
Choose components for the job, not the spec sheet
Adding a 5A boost converter to squeeze more power out of the motor missed the point entirely. The motor spec was fine — the problem was total system weight. More voltage didn't compensate for extra kilograms of battery, converter modules, and wiring. I was solving the wrong problem.
Lesson 02
Weight kills performance faster than anything else
Every part I added had a weight cost. When everything was assembled it was heavier than the motor was designed to push at speed. The car moved, but there was no performance headroom. Stripping the design back on v2 (the small RC car) gave me a much faster car with a simpler circuit.
Lesson 03
Custom remote was worth it
The most complex part of the project was also the most satisfying — building a controller that actually fits your hand, with controls where you want them, communicating the way you designed it. ESP32-NOW is fast enough for RC use and far simpler to implement than a radio module with its own protocol.
