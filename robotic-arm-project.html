What I Learned
  • The AliExpress clone motor-controller board ("Bus Servo Adapter A") passes input voltage straight through to the servo bus with no regulation. The STS3215 needs 4.5–8.4 V; the board accepts 9–12.6 V input. Only safe pairing: a dedicated 7.4V supply — not the 12 V or 5 V I had lying around.
  • My first attempt tapped the servo bus data pin directly, in parallel with each arm's daisy chain. It fought the board's own direction-switching circuit and dropped packets constantly. Going through the board's dedicated UART header (jumper set to UART mode) fixed it instantly — the board already handles half-duplex switching internally.
  • arduino-esp32 v3.3.10 changed the ESP-NOW callback signatures without much warning: receive callbacks now take esp_now_recv_info_t* instead of a raw MAC pointer, send callbacks take wifi_tx_info_t*. Every ESP-NOW example online still shows the old signatures.
  • noInterrupts() does not protect against the FreeRTOS WiFi task pre-empting your main loop — that's not a hardware ISR. Fixed it with a volatile double-buffer instead: the receive callback just copies the packet and flips a flag; the main loop reads it out on its own time.

The SO-101 is TheRobotStudio's open-source leader-follower robotic arm (Apache-2.0) — the current version of their SO-ARM100 design — built for the Hugging Face LeRobot ecosystem — you physically move the leader arm, and the follower mirrors it in real time, the standard way to collect teleoperation data for training robot learning policies. It was chosen over the pricier AM-ARM200 mainly on servo economics — full comparison in the sourcing section below.

Everything through this point is stock SO-101: the 12 Feetech STS3215 servos and both motor-controller boards were bought as parts, the arm links were 3D printed from TheRobotStudio's original files on a Prusa printer, and both arms were assembled and calibrated through LeRobot's Python tooling — homing offsets and safe position limits get burned straight into each servo's EEPROM. The one part that isn't stock: the desk clamps, custom-modelled from scratch — more on that below.

What isn't in the official repo is the wireless link. The arm pair worked over USB from day one; built on top of that are two ESP32-C3 Super Mini boards that read the leader's joints and mirror them to the follower over ESP-NOW, no PC in the loop. Full breakdown in the Wireless Upgrade section below.

Servos, Sourcing & Mechanical

The SO-101 has one real drop-in constraint: the Feetech STS3215 serial bus servo. It's a proprietary 45.2 × 24.7 × 35 mm form factor with position feedback over a single-wire half-duplex bus — no cheaper PWM servo (MG996R and similar) can substitute, because those have no feedback and can't support LeRobot's teleoperation or policy-learning pipeline. The correct variant is the C001: 7.4 V, 19.5 kg·cm torque, 1:345 gear ratio. The 12 V/30 kg C018 variant exists but is more expensive and isn't what the official BOM specifies.

Building a cheaper DIY equivalent — DC motor, gearbox, AS5048 magnetic encoder, custom driver PCB — was seriously considered and ruled out: at roughly $20–30 per joint plus hours of engineering per axis, it lands close to the STS3215's $13.89/unit Alibaba price without any of the reliability or drop-in compatibility.

LeRobot Software & Calibration

Both arms are brought up and calibrated through Hugging Face's LeRobot Python library. A dedicated conda environment (lerobot) was set up with the Feetech servo backend, and both controller boards were found on /dev/ttyACM0 (one at a time, since both boards enumerate identically). Motor IDs were assigned 1–6 on each arm, followed by LeRobot's guided calibration routine, which walks each joint through its range and burns homing offsets directly into servo EEPROM — no recalibration is needed later on the ESP32 firmware, since the offsets travel with the hardware.

Clone motor-controller board — voltage pass-through
The board bought alongside the servos was an AliExpress clone ("Bus Servo Adapter A"), not the official Waveshare board it was modelled on. It turned out to pass input voltage straight through to the servo bus with zero regulation — input range 9–12.6 V in, same voltage out to the servos. The STS3215 wants 4.5–8.4 V (ideally 7.4 V). Fix: power the board from a dedicated 7.4 V supply — not the 5 V or 12 V supplies already on hand. Teleoperation was blocked for a stretch waiting on a second 7.4 V supply for the leader arm.

The Wireless Upgrade

USB teleoperation works, but tethers both arms to a PC and to each other's cable runs — awkward for anything beyond a desk demo. The goal was to cut the cable and mirror the leader arm's 6 joints to the follower wirelessly, with low enough latency that it still feels like direct control.

Phase 1 — PC-Tethered Teleoperation (LeRobot)
Baseline: both arms on USB, driven directly by LeRobot on a PC
The starting point and the one LeRobot ships out of the box: leader and follower both plugged into a PC over USB, with lerobot-teleoperate reading the leader's joint positions and writing them to the follower in a tight Python loop. This confirmed both arms, all 12 servos, and the calibration data were correct before touching any custom firmware.
Phase 2 — First Wired Attempt: Direct Data-Pin Tap
Tapping the servo bus data line directly caused interference with the board's own direction circuit
The first idea was to tap the half-duplex data line of each arm's servo daisy chain directly from a GPIO pin. In practice this fought the motor controller board's own internal direction-switching circuit — the board expects to be the one driving that line's direction, and having an external GPIO also toggling it caused corrupted reads and dropped packets. Fix: go through the board's dedicated UART header instead (with its jumper set to UART mode), which lets the board handle half-duplex direction switching internally — the ESP32 just talks standard UART TX/RX to the header, no data-pin trickery required.
Phase 3 — ESP32-C3 Super Mini Wireless (ESP-NOW) — Working
Two ESP32-C3 Super Mini boards, one per arm, mirror joint positions wirelessly at 100 Hz
The final architecture: a leader board reads all 6 STS3215 positions from the leader arm over UART (1 Mbps, half-duplex) and sends them as a 12-byte ESP-NOW packet. A follower board receives that packet, clamps each joint to safe position limits pulled straight from the LeRobot calibration JSON, and sync-writes all 6 goal positions to the follower arm in one broadcast instruction. Each arm was bring-up tested standalone first — a small servo_test sketch reads and wiggles all 6 joints, confirming the ESP32 can talk to that arm's servos before any ESP-NOW code goes near it. First-time pairing: flash the follower sketch first, read its MAC address off the Serial Monitor, paste it into the leader sketch, then flash the leader. Confirmed working end-to-end — moving the leader arm by hand drives the follower in real time with no perceptible lag.
Confirmed Leader arm mirrors to follower arm wirelessly at 100 Hz — no PC in the loop, no router, ~1–2 ms ESP-NOW round trip.

Electronics & Wiring

Both boards are wired identically. The motor controller board is powered from an external 5 V supply, and the ESP32-C3 talks to it over the board's UART header rather than the raw servo data line.

The ESP32-C3 is powered from USB only while programming, and from the board's 5V rail once standalone. Connecting both simultaneously risks backfeeding one supply into the other — always disconnect USB before powering from the board, and vice versa.

Firmware

Both sketches are plain Arduino C++ using esp_now.h and the STS servo protocol implemented by hand over HardwareSerial — no external servo library. The follower's receive callback runs inside the FreeRTOS WiFi task, so it only copies the incoming packet into a buffer and flips a flag; the main loop does the actual servo write on its own schedule, avoiding any work inside the callback itself.

Next Steps

Teleoperation Data Collection for Policy Training
With wireless teleoperation confirmed, the next step is recording demonstration episodes through LeRobot's data collection pipeline — moving the leader arm through repeated tasks while the follower's joint trajectories are logged, building a dataset toward training an imitation-learning policy.
Camera Integration
Add a camera stream (wrist-mounted or overhead) so recorded episodes include visual observations alongside joint positions — required for most LeRobot policy architectures, which condition on images, not just joint state.
