First hardware project ever

Exhaust cutout valves are butterfly valves fitted in the exhaust pipe — open them and the exhaust bypasses the silencer, giving a much louder tone. Normally these are controlled by a simple toggle switch wired directly to a relay. I decided to put an Arduino in the middle of that, which in hindsight added complexity for no real benefit. But I learned a lot doing it.

The car runs 12V. I needed 5V for the Arduino Nano and a logic-level signal to drive the MD13S motor driver controlling the valve actuator. I used a 3A DC-DC buck converter to step the 12V down to 5V, which was the right call. I also used a voltage divider to get 3.3V for a reference signal — I later confirmed through university coursework that this is not the correct approach for powering a circuit; the output voltage drops under load. A proper LDO regulator is the right answer, and it does work in this project only because the 3.3V line carries very little current.

The enclosure was the part I put the most thought into. Designed entirely in Fusion 360, printed in black PLA — the box has moulded posts so every PCB and module screws in at a fixed position. The top cover has ventilation louvres. The wiring exits through a grommet hole. For a first project, it looked properly finished.

Build

Step 01
Enclosure design in Fusion 360
Modelled a rectangular enclosure with internal mounting posts at exactly the right positions for the Arduino Nano and the MD13S driver. The top cover has horizontal louvres for airflow and a flat mounting face. Designed so all components screw in — no hot glue, no cable ties.
Printed enclosure — base and cover halves
Step 02
Electronics — power circuit and driver
12V in from the car battery → 3A DC-DC buck converter → 5V for the Arduino Nano. The MD13S Cytron 13A motor driver is overkill for an exhaust valve actuator (which draws maybe 1–2A peak), but it was what was available and easy to wire. I also added a voltage divider for a 3.3V reference — as I later learned in university, this is not the correct approach for powering a circuit, but it functions in this project because the 3.3V line draws very little current.
Electronics mounted — MD13S, buck converter, Arduino
Step 03
Arduino Nano — firmware
The Arduino Nano firmware is simple: read a debounced push button input, toggle a boolean state, drive the MD13S direction pins accordingly to open or close the valve. The car DRL red LED lights up when the cutout is open — wired through the same output to give a visual status indicator on the dash without needing a separate LED.
Arduino Nano with wiring
Step 04
Assembly and wiring harness
All boards screwed into the enclosure posts. Wiring exits through a single grommet hole, split into a sleeved harness — power in (12V from fuse box), motor output to valve, and switch/LED wires to dash. The finished unit bolts under the dash or into the boot with two screws through the enclosure flanges.
Finished controller — enclosure, switch, wiring harness

What I'd Do Differently
Motor driverL9110S or small relay — 1–2A is enough
3.3V supplyAMS1117-3.3 LDO, not a voltage divider
MCUATtiny85 — no need for full Arduino
WiringProper connector housing, not bare headers
Enclosure mountDIN rail clips instead of bolt holes
Voltage divider for 3.3V — the wrong call
A voltage divider uses two resistors to create a ratio of the input voltage. It works fine with no load — but as soon as you draw current, the output voltage drops because the resistor values change the division ratio under load. The correct approach for powering a circuit at 3.3V from 5V is an LDO regulator (like the AMS1117-3.3), which maintains a stable output regardless of load. I later confirmed this through university coursework — it's one of those things that works in a low-current situation and hides its own flaw until it doesn't.
MD13S 13A motor driver for a 1A actuator
The MD13S is a 13A capable driver meant for large motors. The exhaust valve actuator draws maybe 1–2A peak. Using a 13A driver here is like using a truck to deliver a letter — it works, but you're carrying around hardware that exists for a completely different application. A simple relay or an L9110S module would have been smaller, cheaper, and less confusing to wire.
The enclosure design was worth the effort
Spending time in Fusion 360 designing proper mounting posts for every component was the best decision of the project. Everything screwed in firmly, nothing shifted during installation, and the finished unit looked like something that belonged in a car. This approach — designing enclosures around components rather than cramming components into a generic box — carried over to every project after this one.

What I Learned

Lesson 01
Voltage regulators, not voltage dividers
A voltage divider is for reading a signal, not powering a circuit. If you need a stable lower voltage under load, use an LDO. This is the kind of thing that seems fine when you test it with a multimeter and no load attached — then fails the moment you connect something real.
Lesson 02
Match components to the actual requirement
An exhaust valve actuator does not need a 13A motor driver. Checking the actual motor current spec before choosing the driver would have saved money and board space. This lesson directly influenced every component selection after this project.
Lesson 03
A designed enclosure is worth the CAD time
The firmware took an afternoon. The enclosure design took two days. The firmware runs quietly in the background — the enclosure is what you see and handle every time you use the thing. Getting it right mattered more than it seemed at the time.
