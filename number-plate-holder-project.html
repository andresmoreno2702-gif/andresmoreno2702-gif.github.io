The goal from the start was a clean plate mount — no visible screws from the front, nothing that looks aftermarket. Four versions later, the design is universal. But the first three needed to exist for the fourth to make sense.

The design point
The constraint wasn't in the problem — it was in the assumption
The first three designs worked perfectly for my plate. The problem was I had hard-coded my plate dimensions into the sketch without realising it was a decision, not a fact. Once I noticed that, the fix was obvious: make the width a variable, not a constant. The final version uses a slot-and-slide mechanism so anyone can set it to their plate size. Same design, same print, any car.

The first bracket — measured the rear plate, modelled a holder to match exactly. Large profile, solid construction. Prototyped in PLA, final prints in black PETG — stronger and more heat-resistant for an outdoor car mount. Fitted correctly and held the plate cleanly.

What I didn't notice yet: every dimension was hard-coded to my specific plate. The design was working, so the assumption felt invisible. You can see this version on the left in the comparison photo — the largest of the three, most material.

Front mesh bracket — screw pressure mechanism
Fusion 360 · Clips into Audi honeycomb grille · M3 screw lock
The front presented a different problem — mounting to an Audi honeycomb grille without drilling any holes. The design clips behind the grille mesh from the inside using two printed feet that wedge between the hexagonal cells. A central threaded post accepts an M3 screw that clamps the whole thing in place, using the mesh itself as the clamping surface.

More mechanically interesting than the rear: the bracket had to be thin enough to clear the grille opening, rigid enough under road vibration, and the screw geometry had to self-align to the mesh geometry. The screw post and T-nut base were modelled as a proper threaded fastener pair — not just a friction fit.

Printed in PETG, installed, still in use. Still fixed to my plate size though.

Rear holder — simplified using front as reference
Fusion 360 · Stripped back · Less material, same function
After designing the front bracket, I went back to the rear with a different perspective. The front had taught me to think about what the geometry actually needs to do — what carries load, what's just material.

Applied that to the rear: cut out everything that wasn't structurally necessary. The slot pattern replaced solid infill, corner bosses stayed because they take the mounting load, the rest was removed. Same clamping action, noticeably less material, faster print.

Still the same plate dimensions. That realisation came later.
Stage 3 installed on the car — simplified clip arms. Old slotted design held for scale comparison.
Stage
04
4
Adjustable universal holder
Fusion 360 · Sliding mechanism · Fits any number plate width
Looked at the Stage 3 design and asked: which dimensions actually need to be fixed?

The answer was none of them. The plate width varies by country and registration type. There's no reason the bracket needs to be sized to one specific plate — the only thing that matters is that the two end clips are the right distance apart.

Solution: split the bracket into two identical sliding halves. Each half has a set of elongated slots. The two pieces interlock and slide freely along their shared axis, then lock at any position when a central screw is tightened through the overlapping slots. The slot grid also reduces material compared to a solid bracket — adjustment and weight saving from the same feature.

Print the same two files, set it to your plate width, tighten. Works for any standard plate size.
